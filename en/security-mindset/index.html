The security mindset

Before any tool, security is a way of thinking. The few habits that stop the vast majority of real attacks.

After this lesson you can
  • Name the habits that stop most everyday attacks
  • Recognise a phishing message
  • Explain why defenders must understand attacks — and where the legal line is

People imagine hacking as clever code breaking through firewalls. In reality, most breaches come from something ordinary: a reused password, an unpatched program, or someone clicking a link. Security is less about tools and more about habits. Here are the ones that matter most.

Four habits that stop most attacks

  • A long, unique password for every account — a password manager remembers them so you don't have to.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere it's offered. A stolen password alone then isn't enough.
  • Keep software updated. Most attacks use holes that were already fixed months ago.
  • Think before you click. Check the real sender and the real link before trusting a message.

Defender first

Everything you learn about attacks has one honest purpose: to defend better. You study how a lock is picked so you can fit a better lock. Learn these techniques only on systems you own or have written permission to test — that line is both the law and the ethic of this field.

Start with a threat model

Security without a plan becomes a pile of products. Before buying or installing anything, answer four questions on one sheet of paper. What do I have that is worth protecting — customer data, money, the ability to keep working? Who might want it — a random robot scanning the internet, a competitor, an unhappy employee? How could they get in — a weak password, an old program, a convincing email? And what would it cost me if they succeeded? The answers tell you where to spend your effort first. A small office and a bank need different answers, but the same four questions.

Notice what is missing from this table: brilliant hackers typing fast in the dark. Almost every real incident starts with one of these six ordinary things, which is good news — ordinary care stops them.

Passwords and two-step login, properly

  • Length beats cleverness. Four random words — 'paper tunnel orange seventeen' — are far harder to guess than P@ssw0rd!, and easier to remember.
  • One password per account. When a website is breached, attackers try the same email and password everywhere else.
  • A password manager makes the two rules above possible: it invents and remembers a different long password for every site, and you remember only one.
  • Turn on two-step login wherever it exists, starting with email — whoever controls your email can reset everything else.
  • An authenticator app is stronger than codes by SMS; a hardware key is stronger still.
  • No real organisation will ever ask for your code. Anyone who asks for it is the attacker.

Updates: the dull habit that wins

When a security hole is announced, a fix usually arrives the same day — and attackers start scanning for machines without the fix within hours. The window between 'fix released' and 'fix installed' is where most break-ins happen. Turn on automatic updates for the operating system, the browser and phones. For servers and network devices, set a fixed day each month, and do not forget the forgotten ones: routers, cameras, printers and the firmware of switches. Anything that no longer receives updates should be replaced or cut off from the internet.

Making a network harder to attack

  • Change every default password — routers, cameras and printers are shipped with passwords anyone can look up.
  • Close what you do not use. Every open port is a door; scan your own network and ask about each one.
  • Never expose remote desktop (3389), Telnet (23) or a device's admin page to the internet. Reach them through a VPN.
  • Separate guests from staff. A visitor's phone should never be on the same network as the accounting computer — that is what VLANs and guest Wi-Fi are for.
  • Use WPA2 or WPA3 on Wi-Fi with a long password, and turn off WPS.
  • Give each person their own account and only the access their job needs. Remove accounts the day someone leaves.
  • Put a firewall between the internet and everything else, with one rule to start from: deny everything, then allow what is needed.

Backups you can actually restore

A backup is the only defence that still works after everything else has failed — against ransomware, fire, theft and plain mistakes. Follow the 3-2-1 rule, and add two more points that people learn the hard way. First, at least one copy must be offline or unchangeable, because ransomware encrypts every disk it can reach, including the backup drive that is always plugged in. Second, a backup you have never restored is only a hope. Once a month, pick a file at random and bring it back; twice a year, restore a whole system.

Noticing an attack: logs

Most victims discover a break-in weeks later, from someone else. Logs are how you find out yourself. On Linux, logins are recorded in /var/log/auth.log; on Windows, in the Security log of Event Viewer. Look for three patterns: many failed logins from one address, a successful login at an unusual hour or from an unusual place, and a new account or scheduled task that nobody remembers creating. The SSH Log Analyzer in the Tools section does the first of these automatically — read its build tutorial to see how.

If it happens anyway: the first hour

  • Stay calm and write down the time and what you see. Notes taken now are worth more than memories later.
  • Disconnect the affected machine from the network — pull the cable or turn off Wi-Fi — but do not switch it off: memory holds evidence.
  • From a clean device, change the passwords of the affected accounts, starting with email and administrator accounts.
  • Tell the people who need to know: your manager, the IT team, the bank if money is involved.
  • Do not pay a ransom in a hurry and do not delete things to 'clean up'. Keep the evidence.
  • Restore from a backup made before the attack, and install every update before reconnecting.
  • Afterwards, find out how they got in and close that door. An incident you learn nothing from will happen again.

The legal line, and how to practise safely

The tools that defenders and attackers use are the same; the difference is permission. Scanning, testing or logging into a system you do not own, without written permission from its owner, is a crime in almost every country — 'I was only curious' is not a defence. Build your own lab instead: two virtual machines on your laptop, an old router, deliberately vulnerable practice systems made for learning, and capture-the-flag competitions. Everything in the free book on this site is meant to be tried there.

Where this leads

Security is not one job but many: the administrator who hardens systems, the analyst who watches logs and investigates alerts, the tester who is paid to break in with permission, and the engineer who designs networks that fail safely. All of them stand on the same base — Linux, networking and a little programming, which is exactly what the other tracks on this site teach. A common order for certificates is Network+ or CCNA for networks, then Security+ for the security basics, and only after that the specialised ones.

Three questions for every system

Professionals judge security with three words. Confidentiality: can only the right people read it? Integrity: can only the right people change it? Availability: is it there when it is needed? A stolen password breaks the first, an altered invoice the second, and ransomware the third. Asking these three questions about any system tells you what to protect first.

  • Least privilege: give each person and program only the access it needs.
  • Layers: a firewall, updates, backups and training together — never rely on one.
  • Backups 3-2-1: three copies, on two kinds of storage, one of them somewhere else. Test that you can restore.
  • A long passphrase beats a short complicated password. Use a password manager and two-step login.
  • Phishing signs: urgency, a strange sender address, a link that goes somewhere other than it says.
  • Never test a system you don't own without written permission. That line separates a professional from a criminal.

Common mistakes

  • Using one password everywhere — one leak opens every account.
  • Postponing updates for months.
  • Having backups but never testing that they restore.

Exercises

Try each one yourself first, then open the answer.

  1. An email says your account will be closed in one hour unless you click a link. Name two warning signs.

    Show the answer
    Urgency, and a link you didn't ask for. Check the real sender address and open the site yourself instead of clicking.

  2. Ransomware encrypts a company's files. Which of the three — confidentiality, integrity, availability — is hit first?

    Show the answer
    Availability. A tested backup is the defence.

  3. What does the 3-2-1 backup rule mean?

    Show the answer
    Three copies, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy kept in another place.

  4. A friend asks you to 'test' the Wi-Fi of a café. What do you answer?

    Show the answer
    No — not without the owner's written permission. Practise on your own lab instead.

Practise this in the lab

Remember
  • Confidentiality, integrity, availability.
  • Updates, strong unique passwords, two-step login and backups stop most attacks.
  • Learn attacks to defend — and only test with permission.
