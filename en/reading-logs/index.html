Reading logs: finding an attack in the noise

Where systems write down what happens, how to read one log line, and the patterns that separate a forgotten password from an attack.

After this lesson you can
  • Find the login, system and web logs on Linux and Windows
  • Read any log line by asking when, where, which program, what and who
  • Tell ordinary noise from the patterns of an attack

What a log is, and why it matters

A log is a diary that a program keeps by itself. Every time something worth noting happens — someone logs in, a service starts, a request is refused — it adds one line with the time and what happened. Nobody reads logs when all is well. But when something goes wrong, the log is the only witness: it tells you what happened, when, and from where. An attacker who gets in will have left traces in it; the question is only whether anyone looks.

Reading one line

PartMeaning
Nov 10 09:40:13When it happened
srvWhich machine wrote it
sshd[902]Which program, and its process number
Failed passwordWhat happened
for rootWhich account was tried
from 203.0.113.45Where the attempt came from

Almost every log line in the world answers the same five questions: when, where, which program, what, and who or from where. Once you look for those five, an unfamiliar log stops being a wall of text.

From thousands of lines to one answer

A busy server writes thousands of lines a day, so you never read a log from top to bottom. You ask it questions with the text tools from the Linux track.

Patterns that mean trouble

PatternProbablyDo
Two or three failures, then success, from a known addressSomeone mistyped their passwordNothing
Hundreds of failures for root, admin, test from one addressA robot guessing passwordsBlock it; use keys and fail2ban
Many failures, then a success, from an unknown addressA password was guessedTreat as a break-in, now
One failure each for many different accountsPassword spraying: one common password tried on everyoneForce strong passwords and two-step login
A login at 3 a.m., or from another countryA stolen accountAsk the owner; reset the password
A new user or a new sudo right nobody asked forAn intruder making a way back inInvestigate at once
Gaps: hours with no lines at allLogging stopped, or the log was cleanedFind out why

The third row is the one that matters most, and the one people miss: failures are noise, but a success after failures is a signal. The SSH Log Analyzer in the Tools section looks for exactly that and prints it at the top.

Web server logs

  • Answer codes: 200 worked, 301/302 moved, 403 forbidden, 404 not found, 500 the server failed.
  • Many 404s from one address for pages you do not have (/wp-login.php, /.env, /admin) is a scanner looking for known weaknesses.
  • Requests containing ../ or ' OR 1=1 or <script> are attempts at attacks.
  • A burst of 500 errors after such a request means the attempt reached something — look closely.
  • awk '{print $1}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head shows the ten busiest visitors.

Making logs trustworthy

  • Correct time everywhere. Without synchronised clocks (NTP) you cannot put events from two machines in order.
  • Send copies to another machine. An intruder with administrator rights can edit the logs on the machine they broke into, but not the copies on a separate log server.
  • Keep them long enough — at least three months. Break-ins are often discovered weeks later.
  • Protect them: logs contain user names and addresses, and only administrators should read them.
  • Decide what deserves an alert. A daily email with 'failed logins: 12, new users: 0' that takes ten seconds to read is worth more than a dashboard nobody opens.

Common mistakes

  • Looking only at failures and never at the success that follows them.
  • Keeping logs only on the machine that may be attacked.
  • Wrong clocks, so events cannot be lined up.
  • Collecting everything and reviewing nothing.

Exercises

Try each one yourself first, then open the answer.

  1. Which command lists the addresses with failed SSH logins, most active first?

    Show the answer
    grep "Failed password" /var/log/auth.log | awk '{print $(NF-3)}' | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn
(In a real line the address is the 4th word from the end, before 'port 51122 ssh2'.)

  2. The log shows 400 failed logins for root from one address and then 'Accepted password for root' from the same address. What do you conclude and do first?

    Show the answer
    The root password was guessed: the server is compromised. Disconnect it from the network, keep the evidence, change passwords from a clean machine and rebuild from a backup.

  3. Your web log has hundreds of 404 answers for /wp-admin, /.git/config and /phpmyadmin from one address. What is it?

    Show the answer
    An automated scanner looking for well-known weak spots. Because they all returned 404 nothing was found, but make sure those paths really do not exist and consider blocking the address.

  4. Why should a copy of the logs be sent to a different machine?

    Show the answer
    An intruder with administrator rights can delete or edit the logs on the machine they control. A separate log server keeps a copy they cannot reach.

Practise this in the lab

Remember
  • Every line: when, where, which program, what, who.
  • Failures are noise; success after failures is the signal.
  • Count and group with grep, awk, sort and uniq.
  • Right clocks, off-machine copies, regular review.
